01

The challenge

A Formula SAE battery pack has to hold as much energy as possible, stay cool while the car is being pushed hard, and pass a strict tech inspection before it’s allowed on track. Every millimeter, gram and degree counts.

As the module’s responsible engineer, I owned its design end to end: the architecture, the busbars, the thermal interface, and making sure it met the rules and could actually be built by the team.

02

Module architecture

I led the redesign of the battery module to make it stronger and lighter per kilowatt-hour. The key move was rerouting inertial loads around the cell cans instead of through them, and I verified the new architecture against the FSAE rules with hand calculations before anything was built. The result was a 30% increase in specific energy, measured in Wh/kg.

Inside the module, I made the busbars slimmer, sized from current and resistivity calculations, and opened up the terminal busbar’s bend radius to cut vertical height. I also staggered the busbar layout so wire bonding was easier and the thermistor data more reliable.

03

Thermal interface & cooling

The cells can’t touch the cooling plate directly, so I designed a thermal interface and spacer system that handles cooling, electrical isolation and assembly fit at once. I used tolerance analysis and material selection to control how much the thermal pads compress, and picked the TIM thickness from a thermal conductivity analysis. That system improved battery performance by 10%.

I then integrated the cooling plates as structural walls of the pack instead of separate parts. That cut cooling-system weight by 11% and the pack’s width by 1.075 inches, with FR4 barriers keeping the cells aligned and electrically insulated through assembly.

04

Validation

Before building anything, I modeled the pack in Simscape to see state of charge, heat generation and cell temperatures under load.

To check the real hardware, I built a cooling-plate validation rig that tests thermal performance under representative cell compression, with Arduino logging of temperature and coolant flow. I validated three plate designs, and all of them landed within 1.2 °C of the CFD predictions.
